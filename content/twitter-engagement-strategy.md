**Template 1: When Someone Complains About Sybil Attacks**

> The core problem with sybil resistance is that most systems force a binary: verified human or not. Real-world trust is contextual. Someone trustworthy enough to comment might not be trustworthy enough to vote on a treasury proposal.
>
> We built zkBaseCred around this idea -- 5 distinct decision contexts (general, comment, publish, apply, governance.vote), each with its own threshold policy. Returns ALLOW / DENY / ALLOW_WITH_LIMITS with a ZK proof. No raw scores exposed.
>
> Live on Base mainnet if you want to look at the contracts.

---

**Template 2: When Someone Discusses DAO Governance Problems**

> The uncomfortable truth about onchain governance: most "one token one vote" systems are already broken, and bolting reputation onto them just creates a different attack surface.
>
> What you actually need is a system where the reputation check itself is verifiable and the raw scores are never exposed (so attackers cannot game to a specific threshold).
>
> That is the design principle behind zkBaseCred's governance.vote context. The proof proves compliance. The decision is on-chain. The underlying scores stay private.

---

**Template 3: When Someone Asks About Reputation Systems**

> Most reputation systems in web3 fall into two categories: (1) output a number (trust score, passport score, ranking) or (2) require some ritual (scan an orb, complete quests, collect stamps).
>
> The problem with numbers is that they are gameable once you know the threshold. The problem with rituals is adoption friction.
>
> There is a third approach: aggregate existing signals, run them through context-specific policy thresholds, and output a decision (ALLOW / DENY / ALLOW_WITH_LIMITS) with a ZK proof that the evaluation was honest. No score revealed. No ritual required.
>
> That is what zkBaseCred does. It sits on top of Ethos, Talent Protocol, and Neynar -- it does not replace them.

**Template 4: When Someone Discusses ZK Proofs**

>
> Instead of revealing "my trust score is 73," you prove "my trust score exceeds the policy threshold for this context" without revealing the actual number. Verifier confirms the proof, gets ALLOW or DENY, never sees the raw signal.
>
> We implemented this with Circom + groth16 in zkBaseCred. The circuit is stable across policy changes -- only the public inputs (policy hash + thresholds) change. Proofs verify on-chain via a DecisionRegistry on Base.

## 3. Thread Engagement Tactics

### 3.1 Engaging in Trending Threads Authentically

**The First Reply Rule**

Being in the first 3-5 replies on a high-visibility thread is extremely high leverage. Monitor Tier A accounts (Jesse Pollak, BuildOnBase, Farcaster founders) and engage within 30 minutes of their posts.

First replies should be:
- Substantive (minimum 2 sentences of genuine insight)
- Never promotional in the first reply
- Conversation-starting (ask a question or offer a counterpoint)

**Bad first reply:** "We built something for this. Check out zkBaseCred."
**Good first reply:** "Contextual trust is the underexplored part of this. 'Should this wallet be allowed to vote on this proposal' is a fundamentally different question from 'is this wallet a real person.' Most reputation systems conflate the two."

**The Follow-Up Play**

After a valuable first reply gets engagement (likes, replies), follow up with a second reply that introduces zkBaseCred naturally:

"We spent 6 months working on exactly this problem. Ended up with 5 distinct decision contexts because 'general reputation' was too blunt an instrument. The governance.vote threshold is meaningfully different from the comment threshold."

**The Thread Contributor Pattern**

When someone posts a long educational thread (5+ tweets), add value by:
1. Replying to the specific tweet where you have the deepest expertise
2. Expanding on their point with an angle they did not cover
3. Tagging the thread author only if your reply is genuinely helpful to them

### 3.2 Strategic Reply Positioning

**Reply to replies, not just OPs.** Often the best conversations happen in reply threads. If someone asks a good question in the replies of a major account's tweet, answering that question thoughtfully gets you visibility with both the OP's audience and the questioner's followers.

**Engage with counter-arguments.** When someone disagrees with a take you align with, replying with a well-reasoned defense (not of your product, but of the technical position) builds authority faster than any promotional tweet.

**The "Thread Tax" Rule:** If you engage in someone's thread, like and retweet the thread itself. This is the social norm. Engaging without amplifying the original author's work signals extraction, not contribution.

### 3.3 Building Conversation Chains That Demonstrate Expertise

**Pattern: Problem > Analysis > Design Decision**

Structure multi-reply conversations to follow this arc:
1. Acknowledge the problem genuinely
2. Add analytical depth (why is this hard?)
3. Share a design decision you made and why (this naturally introduces zkBaseCred)

Example:
- Reply 1: "The sybil problem in QF is structural, not incidental. As long as funding is proportional to the number of contributors, creating fake contributors has positive ROI."
- Reply 2: "Most mitigations try to verify identity. That is necessary but not sufficient. You also need to verify reputation -- has this wallet done anything meaningful, or was it just created to claim this grant?"
- Reply 3: "We ended up building separate decision contexts for exactly this reason. The 'apply' context in zkBaseCred has different thresholds than the 'comment' context because the cost of a false positive in grant applications is much higher."

| Step | Action | Example |

| **Acknowledge** | Confirm you see and understand the criticism. Do not dismiss it. | "Fair point. Let me address this directly." |

**Triage Decision Tree:**

- **Is it technically valid criticism?** Respond with ATRF. Full transparency.
- **Is it a misunderstanding?** Correct gently with evidence. Link to docs or contracts.
- **Is it concern trolling or FUD?** One factual response, then disengage. Do not get into extended arguments.
- **Is it a security disclosure?** DM immediately. Ask them not to disclose publicly. Fix first, communicate after.

### 6.2 "Why Should I Trust This?" Response Templates

This is the most common and most important objection. It must be answered with facts, not reassurance.

---

**Template A: The Trust Architecture Answer**

> You do not need to trust us. That is the point.
>
> The decisions are verifiable on-chain via the DecisionRegistry on Base mainnet. The ZK proofs are generated client-side using the same Circom circuit -- you can verify the proof independently. The policy thresholds are public and hashed into every proof.
>
> What you are trusting: (1) the Circom circuit is correct (auditable), (2) the signal providers (Ethos, Talent Protocol, Neynar) return accurate data, (3) the smart contract correctly verifies proofs.
>
> What you are NOT trusting: us to be honest about what scores we saw. The ZK proof handles that.

---

**Template B: The "Who Are You?" Answer**

> Fair question. zkBaseCred is a reputation primitive built on Base. We are a small team focused on shipping production infrastructure.
>
> What is verifiable: the smart contracts are deployed and readable on BaseScan. The API is live. The ZK proofs verify on-chain. The SDK is published.
>
> We are not asking anyone to trust our brand. We are asking them to verify our code.

---

**Template C: The "This Is Just Another Reputation Score" Answer**

> It is not. Reputation scores output a number. zkBaseCred outputs a decision.
>
> The difference matters: if you know the threshold is 70, you game your score to 71. With zkBaseCred, you never see the threshold, you never see your score, and the evaluator never sees your score either. Both sides see only the decision: ALLOW, DENY, or ALLOW_WITH_LIMITS.
>
> Different primitive. Different attack surface.

### 6.3 Technical Skepticism Response Templates

---

**Skepticism: "ZK proofs are overkill for reputation."**

> If all you need is a public score, ZK is overkill. Correct.
>
> But if you need to prevent threshold gaming (where attackers reverse-engineer the minimum score needed) and you need privacy (where the evaluator does not see raw signals), then ZK is the minimum viable cryptography.
>
> The proof adds ~2.5 seconds to the evaluation. The on-chain verification cost is minimal on Base. The tradeoff is worth it for the security properties.

---

**Skepticism: "You are just wrapping other people's APIs (Ethos, Neynar, Talent Protocol)."**

> Correct. And that is by design.
>
> Ethos, Talent Protocol, and Neynar are signal providers. They are good at collecting and organizing reputation data. zkBaseCred is a decision layer -- it takes those signals, applies context-specific policy thresholds, and outputs a verifiable decision with a ZK proof.
>
> We are not trying to replace the signal providers. We are building the layer above them that protocols actually integrate against.

---

**Skepticism: "What happens if one of your signal providers goes down?"**

> The system is designed to degrade gracefully. If a signal provider is unavailable, the missing signal is treated as zero (worst case), and the decision is computed with the available data.
>
> This means a provider outage results in more conservative decisions (more DENY results), not system failure. The ZK proof still generates and verifies. The on-chain record still gets created.
>
> We chose this approach deliberately: false negatives (denying a legitimate user temporarily) are preferable to false positives (allowing an illegitimate user because we could not check).

---

**Skepticism: "On-chain reputation is a privacy nightmare."**

> Agree -- which is exactly why the decisions use ZK proofs.
>
> What goes on-chain: the decision (ALLOW/DENY/ALLOW_WITH_LIMITS), the context, and the proof. What does NOT go on-chain: the raw scores, the signal sources, or any personally identifiable information.
>
> An observer looking at the DecisionRegistry sees "wallet 0x... was ALLOWED for governance.vote at block N." They cannot determine what scores led to that decision or where the signals came from.

---

**Skepticism: "How is this different from Gitcoin Passport?"**

> Gitcoin Passport is stamp-based. You collect stamps (verification events) and accumulate a score. The score is public. The threshold is known. It answers "is this a real person?"
>
> zkBaseCred is decision-based. It aggregates existing behavioral signals (not stamps), applies context-specific policy thresholds (not one global score), and outputs a decision (not a number) with a ZK proof (not a credential). It answers "should this actor be allowed to do this specific action?"
>
> Different questions, different architectures. Both are useful. They are not interchangeable.
